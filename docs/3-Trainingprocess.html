5 Paradigm

The overarching paradigm in training science is twofold: Minimize injury risk and improve the capacity of function related to the goal. For beginners and less-advanced trainees, we can focus on robust, safe methods to induce adaptation. However, without a training history to refer to, finding the right training load mostly comes down to informed guessing and flexible adjustments. Combined with individual stress outside the training process, beginning trainees show high injury and dropout rates. This should not be the case from a training science perspective. To induce adaptations in beginners, there is no need to push towards the edge of their capabilities, which should (in theory) keep injury risk and overreaching-type states low.

In more advanced athletes, there is a constant tug-of-war between risk minimization and optimal adaptation. Advanced athletes often require increasing training loads to see the desired outcomes. Therefore, risk reduction and improvement must be balanced during the training process. Competition is often accompanied by high uncertainty and uncontrollability (environmental or from competitors), resulting in additional psycho-physiological demands that can take a toll on athletes. The risk of adverse health consequences from training increases alongside the risk of injury.

In general, when working with individual athletes, prioritizing risk reduction is the most pragmatic starting point; an injured athlete cannot express their athletic potential, regardless of optimal performance programming. For non-professional athletes, health improvements are also central, while this is often less discussed for professional or competition athletes. However, even for top athletes, health must be a central parameter. A reduction in general health goes hand in hand with a decreasing potential for recovery and adaptation.

NoteBalance act - minimize risk and optimize adaptation

Always start with low-risk, robust training methods. There is no need to push the trainees’ capacities as long as you see adaptations. Especially for beginners, be brave enough to start with the basics and increase training loads very gradually. There should be no balancing act between risk and reward.

For competition athletes and more advanced trainees, your priorities are methods to reduce the risk of injury and maladaptation. An injured or sick athlete can’t perform. The second step is optimizing adaptations. For advanced trainees, there is sometimes a need to employ training methods with higher risk profiles or training loads that are closer to their psychophysiological tolerance. This can be a viable option as long as it happens athlete-centered, well-structured, and monitored!
